Data visualization and interpretation of results

Alejandro Pascual Hernández · 30 genes · 65 breast cancer patients

Tools R RStudio R Markdown tidyverse ggplot2 patchwork ComplexHeatmap
Objective To apply biological data visualization techniques and statistical analysis to a dataset containing the expression of 30 genes in 65 breast cancer patients, each with different treatments and tumor characteristics, interpreting the results through appropriate charts.

KEY CONCLUSIONS

The study was divided into three parts, with the following conclusions for each:

  • Drug vs. Placebo a significant difference can be seen in the expression of 10 genes in patients depending on whether they were treated with a drug or given a placebo.
  • Distribution differences we looked at how the levels of certain biochemical parameters were distributed across a population and related them to biological implications, such as disease indicators or other conditions.
  • Gene classification and clustering we studied 30 genes of interest, which were grouped into 3 clusters showing the same expression patterns across our patient sample.

                    plot_mki67 + plot_gata3 + plot_foxa1 + plot_ccnd1 +

    plot_layout(ncol = 5)

Looking at the boxplot results, we can see that drug treatment reduces gene expression levels (AQ) to values close to 0. Meanwhile, under placebo, expression values hover around 0.9-1. AQ is a measure of a gene's expression under specific conditions, without direct comparison to another condition. We can therefore interpret that for the 10 genes analyzed, there is a clear difference in expression between when the drug is administered to the patient and when a placebo is given. This may indicate that our drug acts on the same metabolic pathway. These genes may therefore be related to the same biological function, which, if controlled, could help prevent or slow the progression of the disease in question — in this case, breast cancer.

Finally, I'd like to point out that although a chart can be very clear and convincing, as in this case, in our opinion this work would be more complete if we also included a statistical test, such as a Student's t-test (for 2 groups) or a Mann-Whitney U test, depending on whether our data is normally distributed or not.

Task 2: Distribution of Biochemical Parameters (Histograms)

Using a histogram with an added density curve, we'll study how different biochemical parameters are distributed across our study population.

For this second task, we decided to use one of the methods tidyverse offers, pivot_longer(). This method lets us generate all the plots in one go, instead of having to combine them afterwards with patchwork as in the previous task.

# First we define the vector of biochemical variables of interest
  variables_bioquim <- c(
    "glucosa", "leucocitos", "linfocitos", "neutrofilos", "chol", "hdl", 
    "hierro", "iga", "ige", "igg", "ign", "ldl", "pcr", "transferrina", 
    "trigliceridos", "cpk"
  )
  
  # We use a series of pipes (%>%) to first select the variables...
  datos_bio_long <- data_gene %>%
    select(all_of(variables_bioquim)) %>%
    pivot_longer(
      cols = everything(),
      names_to = "variable",
      values_to = "valor"
    ) %>%
    drop_na(valor)

Now we need to combine two types of plots: a histogram and a density plot.

ggplot(
    data = datos_bio_long, 
    aes(x = valor) 
  ) +
    
    # Histogram
    geom_histogram(
      aes(y = after_stat(density)), 
      bins = 30,
      fill = "steelblue", 
      color = "black",
      alpha = 0.7
    ) +
    
    # Density curve
    geom_density(
      color = "red",
      linewidth = 1  
    ) +
    
    facet_wrap(
      ~ variable, 
      scales = "free" 
    ) +
    
    labs(
      title = "Distribución de Variables Bioquímicas",
      subtitle = "Histograma y Curva de Densidad",
      x = "Valores del parámetro (unidad dependiente de cada parámetro)",
      y = "Densidad"
    ) +
    theme_minimal()+
    theme(legend.position = "none",
          plot.title = element_text(size = 20, hjust = 0.5),    
      plot.subtitle = element_text(size = 17,hjust = 0.5),    
      axis.title.x = element_text(size = 17),
      axis.title.y = element_text(size = 17),
      axis.text = element_text(size = 11),
      strip.text = element_text(size = 12, face = "bold"))

Looking at the resulting plots, we can confirm that there are different types of distribution across a population depending on the parameter analyzed. For example, for parameters like transferrin or cholesterol (chol) we could say we're looking at a normal (Gaussian) distribution, or a Student's t distribution. On the other hand, some parameters seem to follow other types of distributions, such as the chi-squared or Fisher's F distribution. This is the case for parameters such as cpk, triglycerides, leukocytes, neutrophils, IgA, IgE and LDL. These are asymmetric distributions clearly skewed towards low values. The clearest case is the CRP parameter.

So, we find two clear types of biochemical parameters. On one hand, parameters that follow a Gaussian distribution are ones where the population typically has certain normal endogenous levels, and both very low and very high levels tend to be considered abnormal or harmful, indicating a possible disease. This could be the case for transferrin, which is responsible for iron transport and whose high or low levels can be linked to diseases and symptoms such as anemia and hemochromatosis (iron overload), respectively.

On the other hand, there are biochemical parameters that tend to stay at constitutively low levels and only rise when there is some alteration or disease. This is the case, for example, of the CRP parameter (C-Reactive Protein), a protein released by the liver during inflammatory processes or infections in the body. CRP levels are normally close to 0 until an infection or inflammation process is triggered. This is also the case for leukocytes and neutrophils, which are immune system cells whose levels are normally low unless there's an attack on the system from viruses, bacteria, wounds, etc.

In short, the shape of the distribution of these parameters tells us the normal levels of a parameter in a population, and which levels we associate with conditions, diseases or anomalies in general. We could now check and relate our breast cancer patients to the biochemical parameters obtained (looking for those that deviated from the mode of their respective distributions) in search of possible markers.

Task 3: Mapping gene expression values (Heatmap)

In this final task, we set out to visualize, using a heatmap, the raw expression of all the AQ variables corresponding to the 30 genes studied in the patients. Initially we decided to use the pheatmap library, but we felt ComplexHeatmap is a more versatile tool that produces more complete and visually better plots.

datos_aq <- data_gene %>%
    select(starts_with("AQ_"))
  
  # We scale the data to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (normalization).
  datos_aq_scaled <- scale(datos_aq)
  
  rownames(datos_aq_scaled) <- data_gene$id
  
  # We transpose the matrix so it's arranged as (Genes x Patients), as required
  datos_final_heatmap <- t(datos_aq_scaled)
  
  library(ComplexHeatmap)
  
  set.seed(1995)
  
  Heatmap(
    datos_final_heatmap,
    
    name = "Nivel de expresión",
    
    column_title = "Heatmap de Expresión Génica en 65 Pacientes",
    column_title_gp = gpar(fontsize = 20),
    
    row_km = 3,
    column_km = 2, 
  
    # Size of the gaps between blocks
    column_gap = unit(5, "mm"), 
    row_gap = unit(3, "mm"), 
    
    column_dend_gp = gpar(col = "grey20", lwd = 3),
    row_dend_gp = gpar(col = "grey20", lwd = 3),    
  

    row_names_gp = gpar(fontsize = 17),
    column_names_gp = gpar(fontsize = 13),
    
    show_row_names = TRUE,
    show_column_names = TRUE,
    
    row_labels = toupper(gsub("AQ_", "", rownames(datos_final_heatmap))) 
  )

First, we can see that there are three large groups, or clusters, of genes based on their expression pattern across the studied population. We'll go through this analysis step by step, focusing on each of these clusters one at a time.

The first cluster we come across (cluster 1, on the left of the heatmap) is made up of the genes from CDH1 to FOXA1. This cluster corresponds to the genes analyzed in Task 1. Within this cluster we can identify three groups of patients: one group whose expression of these genes is very high (between 2 and 1 on the heatmap), colored red (patients from 26-13 to 21-48, left to right). We'll refer to this group from now on as A1 (A=high, 1=cluster 1). Next we find a group with medium expression (from now on M1), with values ranging between 1 and 0, colored a light, almost-white red (patients 10-23 to 17-51). Finally, we have a group with very low expression levels, around -1, colored light blue (the remaining patients), which we'll refer to as B1.

Using the same approach and naming convention, we can identify the patient groups found in gene cluster 2 (from gene EGFR to TNF). Group A2 covers patients 26 to 28 and 21 to 48, and shows high expression. The rest, group B2, shows low expression.

Finally, we have cluster 3 (from gene MYC to HLAA), found at the very bottom, split into a group of patients with low expression of these genes (B3), covering patients 26 through 56; and a group with high expression (A3), covering patients 21 through 4.

With everything laid out like this, we can spot certain patterns even between gene clusters and patient groups. We can see that clusters 1 and 2 are more closely related to each other than to cluster 3. This can be observed both by looking at the branches on the left side and by the fact that patients belonging to group A1 always also belong to group A2, while those in M1 always belong to B2. In other words, there appears to be a correlation between high expression of cluster 2 genes and high expression of cluster 1 genes, and, on the other hand, low expression of cluster 2 genes with medium expression of cluster 1 genes. Cluster 3 genes don't seem to show this kind of correlation with the other clusters.
